LLMs in Scientific Peer Review: A Source-Based Reassessment of 27 Studies

By Richardson Dackam · Independent researcher, Nshipyard
1 October 2026 · Version 0.2.0 · Research report · Not peer reviewed

Abstract

Large language models are increasingly evaluated as scientific peer-review assistants, but studies use heterogeneous outcomes and reference standards. This project asks whether findings from a previous 27-study evidence review persist when newer models independently check the underlying primary sources. GPT-6-Luna and GPT-6.1-Sol were assigned the same fixed corpus and six finding dimensions: agreement, error detection, recall, score bias, overconfidence and limitations. This release contains the original Keenable baseline and independently completed, reviewed 27-study extractions from both Luna and Sol. The completed extraction distinguishes human-comment overlap from verified-error detection, documents score leniency in several designs and identifies substantial variation in error-detection results. These outcomes cannot be pooled across different denominators. All three versions are available in a study-level comparison viewer. Targeted source audits identify identity errors, outcome misclassification and overgeneralization in the baseline, including conflation of synthetic and real errors. This is a reassessment of published evidence, not an experimental replication of reviewer performance or an exhaustive adjudication of every claim. Structured data, sources, locators and corrections accompany the report.

Research question and scope

What empirical studies have compared LLM-generated scientific peer review with human peer review, and what did each study find about agreement, error detection, recall, score bias, overconfidence, and limitations?

The author’s motivation is to verify whether the previous evidence review’s findings still hold under newer evidence-extraction models. The previous review provides the candidate corpus. This reassessment does not perform new peer-review experiments or measure the two extraction models as reviewers.

Methods

The original 27 candidate IDs were retained. Labels and links were treated as unverified inputs; identity mismatches were documented rather than silently substituted. Both agents received the same research question, candidates, cutoff of 1 October 2026 and six-dimensional extraction schema. GPT-6-Luna completed first. GPT-6.1-Sol was instructed to retrieve and extract independently without inspecting Luna’s results. Both used Extra High reasoning.

Use the latest primary-source version available by 2026-10-01, except retain candidate-pinned versions when explicitly fixed in the brief (notably #9, #10, #27). For #13 use the published EMNLP 2025 proceedings paper; for #14 and #20 use latest arXiv v4 and v2; for #21 use arXiv v2/ICML 2026 version. Candidate ID and linked study identity are preserved when labels mismatch.

Each finding records its source and locator and is classified as verified, not measured, not reported or source unavailable. “Verified” denotes a source-checked extraction, not an independent reproduction of the original experiment. Human-comment overlap, rating agreement, synthetic-flaw detection, real-error detection and confidence calibration remain distinct outcomes.

A supervising AI agent reviewed structure and sampled source claims, metadata and metric interpretation. Identified draft errors were corrected. These are reviewed AI-assisted research outputs, not untouched model benchmarks; a full independent human audit is not claimed. The project is a fixed-corpus reassessment rather than an exhaustive literature search, and no protocol preregistration is claimed.

Results and comparison scope

Each extraction contains 27 × 6 cells (162 total). Luna marks 95 verified, 58 not measured and 9 not reported; Sol marks 107 verified, 54 not measured and 1 not reported. These are source-extraction coverage counts, not estimates of model accuracy. The original baseline has 131 populated cells and 31 blanks, preserved verbatim. Source-version and scope differences remain visible.

The following synthesis is drawn from the completed Luna extraction. Candidate numbers refer to the primary-source list below; full findings and locations are available in the Luna matrix.

  1. The evidence separates several different tasks: predicting scores/decisions, matching the topics or comments humans wrote, expert validation of review criticisms, detecting inserted synthetic flaws, and detecting real verified errors. These metrics are not interchangeable.
  2. Score leniency/inflation appears across several distinct designs: e.g., a mean +1.16 rating difference for GPT-5-mini on ICLR/NeurIPS papers (#19), 95–98% acceptance recommendations on a selected set of ultimately published hand-surgery papers (#7), and near-uniform high scores for two models on ICLR 2026 manuscripts (#23). These are not directly comparable effect sizes.
  3. Error-detection results are mixed and misses are common across distinct conditions: SPOT’s best full-set recall was 21.1% on errors verified in published papers (#17); FLAWS GPT-5 top-10 identification accuracy was 39.1% on synthetically inserted fatal errors (#10); and the ICLR 2026 multimodal audit found 12.1% recall under natural prompting and 22.2% with verification instructions for 145 verified injected errors (#23). The post-publication checker identified 60% of 90 controlled injected errors (#18). These benchmarks use different denominators and should not be pooled.
  4. Human comment recall is usually absent or only approximated by topical/point overlap. MARG’s 15.84 comment recall (#6), Beyond Rating’s max-recall matching to expert weakness points (#20), and Nature-paper issue coverage (#24) have different definitions and should not be pooled.
  5. Direct confidence calibration is rare. SPOT reports low self-confidence that is weakly associated with performance, not broad overconfidence (#17). Other studies provide separate evidence of unsupported or unmatched claims: an empty-paper probe elicited invented review assertions from one system (#4); ReviewerGPT’s aggregate table marks two false alarms (#3); MARG failed to prune an invalid comment in 47% of its 30 sampled refinement cases (#6); GPT-4o limitation outputs had 45.9% human-evaluated accuracy on a 100-example sample (#9); SPOT precision was 6.1% against its annotations, although some unmatched flags may be valid unannotated errors (#17); the post-publication checker’s experts confirmed 263/316 flags (#18); and experts rated GPT-5.2 review-item correctness at a paper-level mean of 86.2% (#24). These are not confidence scores and their validation conditions differ. PaperAudit-Bench’s AI-only extra-point rate measures coverage divergence, not invalid claims; the authors say extra points are not necessarily incorrect (#26).
  6. One expert review-item study reports a paper-level mean fully-positive item rate of 60.0% for GPT-5.2 versus 48.2% for a top human reviewer, while paper-level mean correctness was lower (86.2% vs 92.3%) (#24). Aggregate helpfulness or preference results in other work do not establish equivalent factual reliability.

Original baseline and what changed

The original Keenable review covers the same retained 27 candidates with a cutoff of 23 September 2026. The author attributes its generation to GPT-4, but the visible Keenable session and report do not expose generator metadata. This attribution is recorded as unconfirmed; GPT-4 within a study finding identifies a tested model, not the extraction agent.

The three-version comparison displays all 162 cells across the original baseline, Luna and Sol. The baseline’s wording, omissions and possible errors are retained. Its blank cells do not distinguish “not measured” from “not reported.”

  • Candidate #1: helpfulness ratings are distinct from reviewer agreement or paper-score bias.
  • Candidate #7: the source-linked study concerns hand surgery, despite the original cardiology label.
  • Candidate #22: the linked source is a review-gaming study, not the labeled randomized feedback trial.
  • The baseline’s “under half of real flaws” synthesis overgeneralizes: synthetic and real-error targets differ, and several cited detection rates exceed 50%.

See the targeted Luna baseline audit, Luna corrections and Sol corrections. These are source-checked examples, not a claim that every baseline sentence was independently adjudicated.

Corrections to the evidence record

Documented corrections include a candidate labeled as cardiology whose linked study concerned hand surgery; distinctions between synthetic perturbation conditions and acceptance subgroups; and a paper-weighted review-item metric that must not be interpreted as a percentage of papers. AI-only extra review points are not automatically false positives. See the correction log and source notes for the record.

Limitations

Sources use different venues, model versions, prompts, thresholds, datasets and definitions. Several studies are preprints; in-sample decision alignment is not scientific correctness; human comments are incomplete references; synthetic injected errors may not represent natural flaws. No cross-study pooled estimate is justified.

The corpus originates in an earlier review and may omit relevant work. AI extraction can introduce errors even after sampled checking. Both extractions are complete, but the publication has not been peer reviewed. The comparison viewer and targeted audit do not constitute an exhaustive independent adjudication or a model accuracy benchmark; similar wording does not prove correctness. Confidence calibration should not be inferred from unsupported-comment rates.

Conclusion

The reviewed Luna extraction supports a cautious, task-specific reading of LLM peer-review evidence: overlap or perceived helpfulness alone does not demonstrate reliable identification of scientific flaws. The original review’s caution about autonomous reviewing remains consistent with the heterogeneous evidence recorded here, but some original claims require correction. In particular, not all error benchmarks test naturally occurring flaws, and not all reported detection rates fall below half. The retained baseline and side-by-side records support transparent inspection rather than a blanket replication verdict.
