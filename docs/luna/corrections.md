# Corrections and discrepancy log

The GPT-6-Luna matrix was extracted independently from primary sources before consulting the original report. The original artifact’s `/raw` route was then retrieved (HTTP 200; embedded data contains 27 study rows). The points below are specific, source-checked corrections or distinctions; they are not a claim that every sentence in the report was re-audited.

## Verified corrections to the original report

- **Candidate #1 — agreement and score bias.** The report puts identical average helpfulness ratings in the agreement cell, but Robertson’s study measures participant helpfulness ratings, not agreement between full reviews. It also puts helpfulness and formatting reattempts in the score-bias cell; those are not paper-score/acceptance bias. The paper does test synthetic inserted-error detection on 20 papers, so the report’s blank error-detection cell omits that benchmark; its reported recalls belong to synthetic corruptions, not real flaws. Source: [arXiv v1, §§3–4 and Table 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.05492v1).
- **Candidate #2 — recall and outcome labels.** The original baseline correctly transcribes the human-comment recall rates, but the first Luna matrix draft wrongly said no recall denominator was measured. The final NEJM AI article explicitly calls this GPT-4 recall and defines it as the likelihood that a human comment matches GPT-4 output, with rates stratified by reviewer consensus; the matrix is corrected, while retaining the distinction from verified-flaw recall. Two other baseline labels overstate or misclassify results: 65.3% is the share of survey participants perceiving potentially useful feedback on perspectives overlooked or underemphasized by humans, not false-positive criticism; aspect-frequency ratios are comment-topic emphasis, not accept/reject score bias. Source: [NEJM AI final article, pp. 7 and 10, Figs. 2E–F and 3](https://doi.org/10.1056/AIoa2400196).
- **Candidate #7 — source identity.** The report’s row label calls this a “blinded cardiology study,” while the fixed linked URL describes Marrella et al.’s 11-article hand-surgery comparison. The description later in that row correctly mentions hand surgery; the identity label should be corrected to the source-linked study. PubMed records *Hand Surgery and Rehabilitation* 44(4):102225, Epub 2025-07-19, issue date 2025 Sep. Source: [PubMed 40691944](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40691944/).
- **Candidate #14 — source version.** The report links arXiv v3, but v4 (2025-10-08) was available before its stated 2026-09-23 cutoff. The matrix uses v4 under the requested latest-by-cutoff rule. Source: [arXiv v4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.14146v4).
- **Candidate #20 — source version.** The report links arXiv v1, while v2 (2026-04-22) was available by the report’s cutoff. The matrix uses v2. Source: [arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.19502v2).
- **Candidate #22 — source identity.** The report calls the row a “large-scale randomized study of LLM feedback,” but the linked arXiv v1 is Li et al., “Gaming AI-Assisted Peer Reviews Poses New Risks to the Scientific Community,” an abstract-rewriting attack study. It is not the separately described randomized feedback trial. Source: [arXiv v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.10159v1).
- **Candidate #24 — denominator.** The reported 60.0% vs 48.2% fully-positive values are correct, but the denominator is underspecified. Table 4 reports a **paper-level mean fully-positive item rate**, not the percentage of papers. It separately reports raw item-level rates of 58.6% vs 47.8%. Source: [arXiv v1, §3 and Table 4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.20668v1).
- **Candidate #25 — overconfidence interpretation.** The report describes the LLMs as “not overconfident but rather indecisive,” using their recommendation patterns and repeated generic criticisms. The study compares recommendations and feedback but does not measure model confidence calibration. The matrix keeps recommendation leniency under score bias and marks confidence calibration as not measured. Source: [PubMed Central full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13034942/).
- **Candidate #26 — AI-only extra points.** The report uses PaperAudit-Bench’s 46.8% AI-extra-major-points result as evidence for hallucinated or unsupported critiques. The source defines this as divergence from human review coverage and explicitly says additional points are not necessarily incorrect; it does not independently validate those extra points as false positives. Source: [arXiv v1, §5.2 and Appendix B.4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.19916v1).
- **Synthesis — “catch under half of real flaws.”** The cited results combine different targets. ReviewerGPT, FLAWS, PaperAudit-Bench, and the ICLR 2026 multimodal audit use synthetic injected errors (the latter inserts verified errors into real manuscripts); SPOT measures recall on author-acknowledged/retraction-level errors in published papers. The broad wording should preserve that distinction rather than describe every benchmark as real-flaw recall. Sources: [ReviewerGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00622v1), [FLAWS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.21843v1), [PaperAudit-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.19916v1), [SPOT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.11855v1), [multimodal audit](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.28626).
