# Baseline audit: material interpretation checks

This audit compares the completed Luna matrix with the recovered 27-row baseline and verifies material disagreements against the primary studies. It is a targeted source audit, not a row-by-row re-review of every baseline sentence and not a comparison of model accuracy between extraction systems.

## Candidate #2: Liang et al.

The baseline’s human-comment recall figures were correct; the first Luna extraction was wrong to mark recall `not_measured`. In the final NEJM AI paper, Figure 2E–F’s y-axis and caption explicitly define GPT-4 recall as the likelihood that a human reviewer comment matches a GPT-4 comment. The Results section reports Nature-family rates of 11.39%, 20.67%, and 31.67% for comments raised by one, two, and at least three reviewers; ICLR rates are 15.39%, 26.21%, and 39.33%, respectively. The corrected matrix marks this **human-comment recall** as `verified` and expressly distinguishes it from recall of independently verified scientific flaws. [Final NEJM AI article, PDF p. 7, Results “Alignment Between LLMs and Humans on Major Comments,” Fig. 2E–F](https://doi.org/10.1056/AIoa2400196).

The baseline’s interpretation of the 65.3% survey result as “false positive criticisms” is unsupported. The article says 65.3% of respondents thought at least to some extent that GPT-4 offered perspectives overlooked or underemphasized by humans. That is a user perception of potentially novel feedback; the study did not independently adjudicate those additions as wrong or unsupported. The corrected overconfidence cell records no measured confidence calibration or verified false-positive rate and preserves this survey result with its qualification. [Final NEJM AI article, PDF p. 10, “Strengths and Weaknesses of LLM Feedback”](https://doi.org/10.1056/AIoa2400196).

The baseline also files comment-aspect frequency differences under “score bias.” The final article reports 7.27× more comments on implications and 10.69× fewer on novelty from GPT-4; human reviewers request ablations 6.71× more often, while GPT-4 requests experiments on more datasets 2.19× more often. These are comment-topic frequencies, not paper-rating or accept/reject score bias. The corrected cell retains the topical findings while leaving score bias `not_measured`. [Final NEJM AI article, PDF p. 7, Fig. 3 and Results “LLM Feedback Emphasizes Certain Aspects More Than Humans”](https://doi.org/10.1056/AIoa2400196).

The baseline labels this row as a 2023 preprint. The fixed source is a peer-reviewed NEJM AI article published 2024-07-17. Its final version also includes a random sample of 666 eLife submissions with 1,632 reviews and a 425-paper Nature health-sciences subset, in addition to the Nature, ICLR, and prospective-study samples. Matrix metadata and source links now reflect the final article. [Final NEJM AI article, first page and PDF p. 6](https://doi.org/10.1056/AIoa2400196).

## Baseline synthesis: “controlled flaw detection stays under half”

That statement is not supported as an unqualified cross-study threshold. Several controlled **synthetic-injection** point estimates exceed 50%, but they differ in task and denominator:

- **ReviewerGPT (#3): 7/13 = 53.8% paper-level cumulative detection.** Each of 13 constructed short papers had one planted error. GPT-4 received three prompts with three responses per prompt; the paper counted a paper as detected if any response under any prompt succeeded. This is not a single-run success rate or a sample of naturally occurring flaws. [arXiv v1, §3.2 and Table 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.00622v1).
- **PaperAudit-Bench (#26): GPT-5 Fast-mode Error Coverage = 51.4%.** Error Coverage is matched injected gold errors divided by all ground-truth injected errors; the results average across data from eight synthesis models. This is a synthetic benchmark result, not real-flaw recall; macro-F1 was a separate metric, with Gemini-2.5-Pro at 41.4% and GPT-5 at 40.6%. [arXiv v1, §3, “Evaluation Metrics,” and §5.1, Figure 5](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.19916v1).
- **AI Correctness Checker (#18): 60.0% recall on 90 deliberately injected errors.** These errors were placed in 15 copies of five accepted papers, and the checker was run three times per copy. This is an adjacent post-publication paper-auditing system, not a peer-review comparison or a natural-error recall estimate. [arXiv v1, §2.3 and §3.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.05925v1).

Other results sit below 50%, but some are not recall. **FLAWS (#10)** reports GPT-5 top-10 identification accuracy of 39.1% on model-inserted synthetic claim-invalidating errors, not conventional recall. [arXiv v1, §5.2 and Table 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.21843v1). **SPOT (#17)** is a more direct real-error benchmark: its best tested model, o3, had 21.1% ± 4.4 recall on 91 author-acknowledged/retraction-level errors in published papers (83 papers), with explicit author/retraction verification. This is evidence of low performance in that verification task, not a universal estimate for LLM peer reviewers. [arXiv v1, abstract, §§2.2–2.3 and Table 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.11855v1). **Alharbi’s multimodal audit (#23)** tests 145 *verified injected* errors and reports 12.1% detection under natural prompts and 22.2% with explicit verification instructions; “verified” describes the planted errors, not naturally occurring flaws. [arXiv v1, §§3.2–3.3, Figures 2–3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.28626v1).

**Verdict:** the high-level concern that error detection often misses flaws is supported, including low recall on SPOT’s author-verified published-paper errors. The precise slogan “stays under half” is contradicted by the cited 53.8% and 51.4% controlled-benchmark values and by the checker’s 60.0% injected-error recall. Those values cannot be pooled or read as evidence of general reviewer accuracy. The replacement synthesis should name the benchmark type and metric for each result, and avoid a universal threshold.
